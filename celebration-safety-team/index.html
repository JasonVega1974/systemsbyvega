Quick-reference response protocols. Based on published guidance from CISA, FEMA, Ready.gov, NCMEC, the American Heart Association, the Red Cross and the CDC — adapt details to this facility with your Team Lead.
🚑 In any life-threatening emergency, call 911 first. Nampa Police non-emergency: (208) 465-2257 · Church office: (208) 466-5433

This is the emergency this team is most likely to face, and it is the one where minutes decide the outcome. Call 911 first, then send someone for the AED. Two people move: one calls, one fetches.

📍 AED locations are not yet recorded. Every team member must be able to reach the nearest AED without thinking about it. This is an open item — the positions go on the site schematic and in this procedure as soon as the church supplies them.

Someone collapses and is not responsive and not breathing normally — cardiac arrest

  1. Unresponsive, and not breathing or only gasping? Treat it as cardiac arrest. Gasping is not breathing — see the box below.
  2. Activate 911 and get the AED — together, not in sequence. If others are with you, one calls while another runs for the AED. If you are alone: call first, then start CPR. With a phone, put it on speaker and do both.
  3. Start CPR. "Press hard. Press fast in the center of the chest." Untrained is not a reason to stand back — doing nothing is the only certain mistake.
  4. Kneel down beside them so their chest is at about the level of your knees, and work where they lie. Do not drag someone to a "better" spot — moving them costs more than the firmer floor gains.
  5. Apply the AED pads and follow the prompts. It will not shock a heart that does not need one.
  6. Swap compressors about every 2 minutes if enough of you are there. Good compressions get shallow faster than anyone expects.
  7. Clear a path for paramedics, hold the doors, and have someone meet them in the parking lot.
🩺 "They're still breathing" is the mistake that kills. Slow, irregular, snoring or gasping breaths are agonal breathing — a sign of cardiac arrest, not against it. It shows up in 40–60% of cardiac arrests, and the AHA names it as a common reason bystanders wrongly decide someone is not in arrest. If you are unsure, compress: the risk of doing CPR on someone who turns out not to need it is low, and the benefit to someone who does is enormous.
Technique, for anyone who wants the numbers. Heel of one hand on the lower half to lower third of the breastbone, other hand on top, fingers overlapped. Rate 100–120 per minute. Depth at least 2 inches (5 cm), not more than 2.4 inches (6 cm). Let the chest come all the way back up between compressions — don't lean on it. Trained in full CPR? Add breaths, 30 compressions to 2 breaths. Source: 2025 AHA Guidelines, Part 7: Adult Basic Life Support.
💊 If an opioid overdose is suspected, the 2025 lay-rescuer algorithm adds one step: give a dose of naloxone if it is available. It does not replace CPR — compressions continue. Whether this church stocks naloxone is an open question for leadership.

Using the AED — Red Cross steps. These apply to an adult, or a child aged 8 or older weighing more than 55 pounds.

  1. Complete the CHECK and CALL steps first.
  2. As soon as the AED is available, turn it on and follow the voice prompts.
  3. Bare the chest and wipe it dry if needed. One pad upper right, the other lower left a few inches below the armpit. If the pads would touch, one on the centre of the chest and one on the back between the shoulder blades.
  4. Plug in the pad connector cable if it is not already.
  5. Let it analyse — nobody touching the person. Say "CLEAR!" out loud.
  6. If it advises a shock: again make sure nobody is touching, say "CLEAR!", press the shock button.
  7. Immediately resume CPR after the shock, or straight away if no shock is advised.
👚 Do not let modesty cost a life — and do not make it harder than it is. Women are defibrillated by bystanders less often than men, and hesitation over clothing is part of why. The 2025 AHA guidance is that it "might be reasonable to adjust the position of a bra instead of removing it." Move it out of the pads' way and carry on. Screen the person with bodies or a coat if you can spare someone — but never delay the shock to arrange privacy.
📚 Red Cross — AED steps

Seizure — most seizures are not emergencies, and the wrong help causes the injuries. Stay. Safe. Side.

  1. Ease them to the ground if they are falling. Time it — the clock is the decision.
  2. Turn them gently onto one side, mouth toward the ground, to keep the airway clear.
  3. Clear hard objects and chairs out of the space. Something soft and flat under the head. Take their glasses off. Loosen anything tight at the neck.
  4. Stay with them, keep a screen of people back, and stay calm until they are fully alert.
Call 911 if: the seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes · another seizure follows soon after · they have trouble breathing or will not wake up afterwards · they were injured · it happened in water · it is their first-ever seizure · they have diabetes and lose consciousness · they are pregnant. On a Sunday, add one more: if it is a visitor you do not know and nobody is with them.
🚫 Do not hold them down or try to stop the movements · do not put anything in their mouth (it breaks teeth and jaws; they cannot swallow their tongue) · do not give rescue breaths during the seizure · do not offer food or water until they are fully alert.
📚 CDC — First aid for seizures · Epilepsy Foundation — tonic-clonic first aid

Falls — the most common injury on a Sunday, especially among older members, and worst in the parking lot from November to March.

  1. Do not help them up. The instinct is to lift someone off the floor, and it is how a hip fracture or a spinal injury becomes permanent. Kneel down to them instead.
  2. Tell them not to move and to answer your questions out loud. Keep them in the position you found them unless you need to give CPR or control bleeding.
  3. Call 911 for any of: a fall from higher than their own height · head, neck or back pain or visible injury · confusion or a change in alertness · weakness, tingling or numbness · bruising around the eyes or behind the ears · blood or fluid from the nose or ears · trouble breathing, seeing, or balancing.
  4. If they are not hurt and want to get up, let them do it slowly and in their own time — roll to the side, rest, onto hands and knees, then up using a sturdy chair. Do not rush them.
  5. Keep them warm, stay with them, and write the incident up the same day — including near-misses and the lot conditions.
📚 Red Cross — head, neck & spinal injury · CDC — preventing falls. Prevention is team work too: salted walkways, dry entry mats, no trailing cables, lights that work.

Federal guidance (DHS/CISA/FBI) gives three options to consider, listed here in the order they are generally preferred. CISA deliberately frames these as options rather than a fixed 1-2-3 sequence: you take the one the situation actually allows, and that decision belongs to whoever is in the room.

  1. RUN — if there is an accessible escape path, evacuate. Leave belongings, help others if possible, keep hands visible, and get people moving away from the threat.
  2. HIDE — if evacuation is not possible, get out of the shooter's view. Lock/blockade doors, silence phones, stay quiet and behind solid objects.
  3. FIGHT — as a last resort and only when your life is in imminent danger, act with physical aggression and improvised weapons to incapacitate the shooter.

When law enforcement arrives: keep hands visible and empty, follow all commands immediately, avoid pointing or yelling, and do not stop officers to ask for help — they will move toward the threat first.

Call 911 when safe. Give location, number/description of shooter(s), weapons, and number of potential victims.

LOCKDOWN! Locks, lights, out of sight. Called when the danger is inside — an intruder, a custody dispute turning aggressive, a violent person in the building. One of FEMA's five required functional annexes for a house of worship.

Lockdown is not evacuation and it is not Secure. Secure means the threat is outside and the walls are protecting us, so ministry continues. Lockdown means the threat is inside and ministry stops.

  1. Announce it plainly over the team channel and the PA: "Lockdown! Locks, lights, out of sight." Repeat it. Do not use code numbers — visitors do not know them, and neither do arriving officers.
  2. Lock interior doors. Lights out. Everyone away from sight lines — away from door glass and windows, behind solid furniture. Silence phones, do not just mute the ring.
  3. Do not open the door. Not for a knock, not for a voice, not for someone in a uniform. Law enforcement will open doors themselves or identify through the door with dispatch confirmation. This rule is what makes a locked door worth anything.
  4. Account for people. Every room leader counts who is with them and reports missing, extra, or injured people to the Team Lead.
  5. Prepare to evade or defend. If the threat enters the room, lockdown has already failed for that room and the options in the Active Shooter procedure apply.
  6. Call 911. Give the building address, which part of the building, what you saw, and how many people are with you. Stay on the line if it is safe.
🧒 Children's areas lock down first and unlock last. A child released into a hallway during a lockdown is the outcome this entire procedure exists to prevent. No child leaves a room, and no adult collects one, until a Team Lead or law enforcement gives the all-clear in person.

Code Adam is the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children's free missing-child protocol. NCMEC reports it is in use in hundreds of thousands of locations nationwide. NCMEC's six steps, in order:

  1. Get a description. Name, age, gender, hair and eye colour, height, weight, and what the child is wearing — including shoes (clothing can be changed quickly; shoes usually aren't). Get a photo if the guardian has one on their phone.
  2. Page "Code Adam" with the description over the team channel to all team members and staff.
  3. Begin the search. Assigned team members cover every exterior door and the parking lot — no child leaves unaccompanied — while others sweep the building: sanctuary, kids' rooms, restrooms, hallways. Escort the parent or guardian to the main entrance so they can help identify the child on sight.
  4. Call 911 immediately. Do not wait, and do not finish searching first. The search and the call happen at the same time.
  5. Locate the child. If found safe and unharmed, reunite with the verified parent/guardian and check the claim tag. If found with someone other than a parent or guardian, use reasonable, non-violent efforts to delay their departure and note description, vehicle, and direction of travel for police.
  6. Conclude the incident. Announce "Code Adam cancelled" so the building stands down, then report the incident to church leadership — including false alarms, which are the cheapest training data the team will ever get.
⏱️ There is no waiting period. NCMEC's instruction is "DON'T HESITATE, CALL 911 IMMEDIATELY." Any rule of thumb that sounds like "search for ten minutes first" is not part of Code Adam and must not be used here.

🔑 Normal pickup and emergency reunification are different things. Sunday check-in tags are a convenience system. Reunification is a chain-of-custody system: a controlled, accountable change of custody from the church to a verified parent or guardian. Improvising it, in the Standard Reunification Method's words, ignores "the responsibility … to maintain the chain of custody for every student."

The one thing that cannot be negotiated: 100% accountability.

  • Class leaders take a head count as they exit, not at the assembly area.
  • Counts go to one person, who knows the total who were on site.
  • Some people will leave on their own. Expect it, especially in a violent incident. Absent does not mean injured — but you must be able to name who is unaccounted for, not just count them. "We are missing 6" is useless to a fire officer; six names is not.

The flow. Keep the children out of the parents' line of sight — that single decision is what keeps the process orderly.

  1. Parent check-in point — somewhere clearly separate from where the children are gathered.
  2. Children assembled elsewhere, with their class leaders, beyond the parents' field of vision.
  3. Greeter meets parents, explains the process, hands out a reunification card. Our friendliest people belong in this role.
  4. Checker verifies ID against the emergency contact list, splits the card, hands the parent the bottom half.
  5. Reunifier takes that half to the children's area, collects the child, and brings them out — and asks the child, "Are you okay going home with this person?"
  6. Exit — one person collects the slip, records the child as released, and says goodbye. This is where the count closes.

One of FEMA's five required annexes, and the one most often confused with the others. This is for a hazard in the outside air — a chemical release, a tanker or rail incident, heavy smoke. It is not the tornado procedure and not Secure.

Three different things, three different actions:
Secure — threat outside, doors locked, ministry continues. Air is fine.
Shelter (tornado / earthquake) — structural hazard; move to the right room, brace.
Shelter-in-place (this card) — the air is the hazard. Seal the room.

Deciding: stay or go? FEMA states the trade-off plainly — "sheltering-in-place is best used when evacuating the public would cause greater risk to them than staying where they are, or when an evacuation cannot be performed due to time or other constraints." And against it: "Evacuation may be preferable when the substance released includes flammable vapors, the substance will linger for a long time in an area, or buildings cannot be closed tightly enough to provide safe shelter."

In practice, follow what emergency officials tell you. Where there is no instruction yet and the plume is close, sealing a room beats driving through it.

Sealing the building

  1. Everyone inside. Close and lock all exterior doors and windows.
  2. Shut down the air handling. Turn off air conditioning and ventilation, or set the system to 100% recirculation so no outside air is drawn in. Close vents and any fireplace damper. Somebody must know how to do this before the day it matters.
  3. Move to an interior room with as few openings as possible, above ground level. Many chemical vapours are heavier than air, so higher is better and basements are worse.
  4. Seal it. Plastic sheeting and duct tape over doors, windows and vents. Also seal around air conditioning units, bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans, and stove and dryer vents. Wet towels along the bottoms of doors.
  5. If vapour may already be inside, breathe shallowly through a cloth or towel.
  6. Do not eat or drink anything that may have been exposed.
  7. Monitor local radio or official alerts. Stay sealed until officials say it is over — people leaving a shelter too early is a known way this goes wrong.

Most incidents at churches are resolved with calm words, not force. Core principles taught by the Crisis Prevention Institute (CPI), Verbal Judo, and church-security trainers:

  • Be empathic and listen actively — let the person vent; acknowledge feelings without agreeing to demands ("I can hear that you're upset — let's figure this out").
  • Respect personal space — CPI says stand at least 1.5 to 3 feet away from someone who is escalating; crowding raises anxiety. Keep non-threatening body language: hands visible, relaxed posture, calm and low tone of voice.
  • Ask open-ended questions and offer choices ("Would you rather step outside to talk, or sit down over here?") — choices restore a sense of control.
  • Set clear, simple limits and allow silence and time for decisions. Avoid commands like "calm down," arguing, or matching their volume.
  • Work in pairs; one talks, one observes. Position between the person and the congregation with an exit route for both of you.
  • If de-escalation fails or a weapon/threat appears, disengage to protect people and call 911. Safety over resolution — every time.

Most bomb threats arrive by phone, and most prove to be false — but CISA's guidance is that they are serious until proven otherwise, and that houses of worship are among the most commonly targeted places.

⚠️ Do not assume "evacuate immediately". CISA does not say that, and evacuating blindly can move people toward a device. The response depends on an assessment of the threat, made by a designated Decision Maker against criteria written down in advance. Celebration Church has not yet named that person or written those criteria — both are open items, and this procedure is incomplete until they exist.

If you take the call

  1. Stay on the line and keep them talking. Do not hang up, even if they do.
  2. Work through the bomb threat checklist — where is it, when will it go off, what does it look like, why. Write the caller's exact words.
  3. Note the voice, the background sounds, the language used. Note the number that called.
  4. Signal a second person to alert the Team Lead and call 911 while you stay on the line.
🖨️ Print the checklist and put a copy by every phone that takes outside calls — the office phones first. Nobody composes good questions from memory mid-threat. CISA Bomb Threat Checklist (PDF, 1 page)

Assessment drives the response — CISA's four tiers:

  • Minimal — assessed and discounted on experience (a repeat caller with no new or specific detail). Monitor.
  • Low — lock down so nothing new comes in. If a suspicious item turns up, the threat escalates.
  • Moderate — lock down and search, fully or partially. Again, a suspicious item escalates it.
  • High — specific and realistic. Partial or full evacuation.

If a suspicious item is found

  1. Do not touch it. Do not move it. Not to check, not to be sure.
  2. Do not use a radio or mobile phone near it. Move well away first, then call.
  3. Call 911. A suspicious item is no longer an assessment question.
  4. Move people away from the item, not past it, and keep the route clear for responders.

Smoke days are a normal part of a Treasure Valley summer, and they are far more likely here than most of the hazards on this page. The people it hurts first are the ones already in the building: older members, anyone with asthma, COPD or heart failure, pregnant women, and children.

Check the AQI before a smoke-season Sunday at AirNow.gov. The categories:

  • Good, 0–50 — no action.
  • Moderate, 51–100 — fine for almost everyone; unusually sensitive people may notice symptoms.
  • Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups, 101–150 — short outdoor activity is fine. Shorten and ease longer outdoor activity, and take more breaks.
  • Unhealthy, 151–200 — move longer or more strenuous outdoor activity indoors, or reschedule it.
  • Very Unhealthy, 201–300 — move all activities indoors or reschedule.
  • Hazardous, 301+ — emergency conditions for everyone. No published schools-style threshold goes this high; this is a leadership call about whether to gather at all.

What the team does on a smoke day

  1. Move outdoor greeting and parking-lot posts inside or under cover, and rotate those posts more often.
  2. Keep exterior doors closed between services rather than propped — every propped door is the filtered air leaving.
  3. Indoors is only cleaner if the HVAC is actually filtering. A well-maintained, properly fitted filter on a forced-air system lowers indoor particles; a propped door or an unfiltered system does not.
  4. Watch for people struggling: coughing, wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, unusual fatigue. Ask, sit them down, and treat a real breathing problem as a medical emergency.
  5. Anyone with asthma should have their reliever inhaler on them, not in the car.

Continuity planning keeps ministry running after an emergency (fire, flood, violence, loss of facility). Following FEMA's continuity guidance, the church plan should identify:

  • Essential functions — worship services, children's ministry, communications, giving/finance, pastoral care.
  • Orders of succession & delegations — who leads if the Senior Pastor or a key staff member is unavailable.
  • Alternate facilities — pre-identified worship/meeting locations and an online service option.
  • Communications — how staff, team, and congregation are notified (call tree, text, email, social).
  • Vital records — insurance policies, member data, and financial records backed up off-site.
